Section 02

It already understands the machine

A single working session was spent reading everything available about the unit and building a structured engineering model of it.

The starting material was four documents: a research proposal, a technical report, a technical presentation, and the electrical construction drawings. None contained a process flow diagram or P&ID for the machine being built. The process was reconstructed instead from the instrument tag numbering inside the electrical package — the tag sequence encodes the flow path, and the control system descriptions name every major item of equipment.

The recycle branch is the heart of the process. A charge circulates through steps 2 to 5 repeatedly until it meets specification, which means the number of passes — not the flow rate — is the primary operating variable, and the one that most directly determines both product value and throughput.
Why the instrumentation matters

The skid carries six flow meters with totalisers, pressure and temperature both entering and leaving the cavitation device, and an inline viscosity transmitter on the outlet — the natural measure of whether a batch is finished. Seven spare measurement channels are available at the control panel. This is a better-instrumented unit than most of its size, which is what makes systematic improvement achievable rather than theoretical.

Section 03

Where production is currently left on the table

The unit has one dominant operating decision, and it pulls against itself.

The unit is built around a premise: each pass through the cavitation device upgrades the oil further, so a charge is recirculated until it meets specification. Every pass also burns energy, occupies the machine and consumes equipment life. If the premise holds, more passes means better oil and less of it.

How much each pass actually delivers on this feedstock has never been measured. That measurement is the first thing the work below produces, and everything else depends on it.

Illustrative, not measured. The shape is characteristic of this kind of process — value rises with diminishing returns while cost rises steadily, so margin peaks and then falls. Where the peak sits for this machine on this feedstock is what the commissioning work establishes.

Short of the peak, oil leaves the plant worth less than it could have been. Past it, machine time and power are being spent producing quality that does not earn its cost.

The peak is not a fixed setting. It moves — and a unit run on habit cannot track a target that moves.

1
Feed quality — varies batch to batch
2
Machine condition — drifts as it wears
3
Crude price spread — moves constantly
4
Feed waiting — decides if machine time is scarce

This is the largest recurring opportunity the unit presents. It repeats on every batch, for the life of the equipment, and the same measurements that answer it also answer where the batch cycle loses time and which step limits throughput.

Section 04

How the profitable operating point gets calculated

Not a principle — a model, with defined inputs. Here is its structure and what each input requires.

margin per batch =
  (volume out × price at achieved quality)
  − (passes × cost per pass)

optimum = the pass count where the next pass stops paying for itself

Two of the five inputs are already known to the business — your sales terms and whether feed is waiting. A third needs a cable and a drive setting rather than new equipment: the variable-speed drives carry an unused analog output and the control system has spare input channels. The remaining two come from lab samples taken while the unit is being commissioned.

Making product value visible in real time

Upgraded crude is typically priced on gravity, and the skid cannot measure gravity. It does measure viscosity, continuously, at the cavitation outlet.

Taking lab samples at intervals through the first batches establishes the relationship between the two. After that, an instrument already bolted to the machine becomes a live readout of what the batch in the tank is currently worth — and the operator can see, while running, how close the charge is to the point where further processing stops paying.

It costs some lab work during commissioning and nothing afterwards.

Section 05

The compounding loop

The value is not a one-off analysis. It is a cycle that tightens each time it runs.

Step 01
Measure
Every batch logged in full — conditions, pass count, energy, outcome.
Step 02
Find the pattern
Which variables actually moved the result, separated from noise.
Step 03
Change one thing
A single deliberate adjustment, with the expected result written down first.
Step 04
Compare
Outcome against prediction. Where they differ, the model was wrong and improves.
↺  each pass through the loop makes the next recommendation sharper

Most units never close this loop. They accumulate operating data for years without a structure that turns it into knowledge, and improvement stays a matter of opinion.

The difference between a machine that has run for a year and a machine that has learned for a year is entirely in whether this loop was running.

Horizon one
Make it legible
First months of operation. Establish what the machine actually does, as fact rather than expectation.
Horizon two
Run it at the optimum
Ongoing. Calculate the most profitable operating point and keep it current as conditions move.
Horizon three
Make it repeatable
At scale. Convert one working machine into a design that can be built, sold and supported again.
Each horizon depends on the one before it. The measurements taken in the first months are what make the second possible, and the evidence accumulated across the second is what makes the third credible to a buyer.
Section 06

What it delivers, over time

Horizon one · first months of operation
Establish what the machine actually does

Before anything can be improved, the unit’s behaviour has to be established as fact rather than expectation.

  • A characterised baseline — the documented reference every future change is measured against. Without it, no improvement can be proven, only asserted.
  • The uplift-per-pass curve — how much each additional cycle genuinely delivers, and where the returns flatten. This is the curve that answers Section 03.
  • Product value visible live, through the viscosity relationship described above.
  • The bottleneck identified — where the batch cycle loses time and which step limits throughput.
  • Instrument integrity confirmed — drift and faults caught before they corrupt months of conclusions.
Horizon two · ongoing operation
Operate at the most profitable point, continuously

Once the curves are measured, the optimum is calculated rather than guessed — and recalculated as conditions move.

  • Pass count set against margin, not habit. The point where an additional pass stops paying is computed from measured data and updated as inputs change.
  • Operating targets that track the market. When the heavy-to-light price differential widens, additional processing pays; when it narrows, it does not. The target moves with it rather than lagging it.
  • Treatment matched to the oil actually arriving, rather than fixed to an average that may rarely occur.
  • Throughput raised by removing the limiting step, identified from cycle data rather than impression.
  • Predictive maintenance. Cavitation is inherently erosive; tracking device condition converts unplanned failures into scheduled work.
  • Autonomous monitoring — routine analysis running against live data, surfacing only what needs a decision.
Horizon three · multiple units
Turn one working machine into a repeatable product

Selling or replicating units requires something a single working machine does not automatically produce: a design that can be handed to someone else.

  • The documented, transferable design package. A machine cannot be sold, warranted or manufactured repeatably from a design that exists only as one physical object. Assembling that package is a direct output of the work above.
  • Performance evidence that survives technical diligence — measured, sourced and reproducible, so what is presented to a buyer or lender holds up when examined.
  • A known performance envelope across feedstocks, so what can be promised to a customer is known rather than hoped.
  • Standardised commissioning, so each new unit is brought online against a proven protocol instead of being worked out again.
  • Fleet monitoring. Comparing units against each other turns every installation into evidence about all of them — a problem found on one becomes a fix applied to all.
  • The accumulated engineering record as an asset in itself — part of what a buyer, partner or lender is actually being offered.
Beyond the upgrader

The method is not specific to this machine. Any operation where a controllable variable trades against cost — and where the right setting moves with market conditions — can be modelled the same way. The upgrader is the first application, not the only one.

Section 07

The capability grows as the system is understood

Everything above is written from a standing start — four documents, no operating data, and a machine that has not yet run.

The current model was built entirely from paper. Ordinary questions about the unit remain open: what the equipment is rated for, what the instruments measure, what the incoming oil looks like. As those are answered, and as the machine begins producing live data, both the depth of the model and the range of what can be done with it expand together.

The relationship is direct. Better information produces sharper analysis; sharper analysis produces better questions; live operating data converts reasoning about a machine into measurement of one. What is described here is a floor, not a ceiling.
